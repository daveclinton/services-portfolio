I talk to ChatGPT every day. One thing I like about voice is that I can start explaining something before I have worked out exactly how to ask it. I can pause to find a word, change direction halfway through, or say, “Wait, that's not quite what I meant.”

When the app lets me do that, talking feels easy. When it answers while I am still thinking, or keeps going after I interrupt, I notice immediately.

That everyday experience is the voice AI problem I find interesting. What has to happen for software to hear a messy, changing thought and respond at the right moment?

## What developers mean by voice

“Voice AI” can mean several different things. **Speech recognition** turns audio into words. **Text-to-speech** reads words aloud. Put those around a language model and you have a basic voice assistant: listen, transcribe, decide what to say, then speak. This is often called a *chained* pipeline. [OpenAI’s voice-agent guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-agents) describes it alongside models that handle the conversation more directly.

The chain is useful because you can inspect the words at every step. If I say “Tuesday” and the transcript says “Thursday,” a developer can see where the mistake happened. The cost is that each stage takes time, and a transcript cannot carry every pause, tone of voice, or half-finished thought.

A live voice model takes in audio and produces speech as the conversation unfolds. Some are **full duplex**: they can listen while speaking, so you can interrupt them. That phrase sounds technical, but the difference is ordinary. If the app is answering and I say, “Wait, go back,” I expect it to stop and hear me.

Even then, the model needs a sense of when your turn is over. A pause may mean you are thinking, or it may mean you are finished. [OpenAI’s voice activity detection guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-vad) describes how a realtime app detects speech starting and stopping. [ElevenLabs lets developers tune](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-agents/customization/conversation-flow) how eagerly an agent takes its turn and whether users can interrupt it. These settings sound small until an assistant starts answering while you are still trying to finish a sentence.

## How far has it reached?

I have put these companies in the order of the questions they raise for me. Block starts with a speech-only research experiment. OpenAI comes next because ChatGPT is the voice app I use. ElevenLabs shows how a developer can assemble the pieces. Qwen then adds what a camera can see, ByteDance looks closely at conversational timing, and MiniMax brings us back to how the answer sounds. It is a way through the technology, not a ranking of the companies.

### Block and Dessa

Jack Dorsey’s Block announced [Dessa](https://block.xyz/dessa) this month. Block says it trained the model on recorded human conversation, with no written text or pretrained language model. It describes Dessa as able to listen and speak at the same time. In Block’s recordings, it laughs or makes a small sound of agreement while the other person is still talking. The company says it can run locally on a MacBook Pro with an M4 Max chip or better.

That is why I start here. A written transcript of a voice chat preserves the words, but hesitation, emphasis, and tone also carry meaning. Dessa is an attempt to learn from the sound itself. I find the idea exciting. I also have to keep its current status clear: Block calls it a research preview, offers early access to a small group of safety researchers, and has not published a public API or independent evaluation on that page. A recording lets me hear a possibility; it does not tell me how the model performs over hundreds of ordinary conversations.

### OpenAI and ChatGPT Voice

After that research question, I come back to something I actually use: [ChatGPT Voice](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/features/voice). I talk to it daily, and the moments that matter are small ones. Can I pause without losing my turn? Can I correct myself while it is speaking? Can I continue a thought without starting a new conversation? The [GPT-Live model](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-agents) behind the current voice experience can listen and speak at the same time.

OpenAI’s [developer guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/audio) lays out several ways to make an app talk. GPT-Live can manage the spoken exchange while a separate backend handles longer work and tools. The Realtime API can handle speech, reasoning, and tools in one session. Transcription and text-to-speech are available separately for a chained design. These choices matter because a voice that responds naturally still needs an application behind it to check facts, enforce permissions, and finish a task.

### ElevenLabs

I put [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-agents/overview/) after OpenAI because its products make the parts of a voice agent especially visible. ElevenAgents can coordinate speech recognition, a language model, speech generation, and turn-taking. Developers can also use its [Speech Engine](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/speech-engine) to add a voice interface to an agent they already have, or use ElevenLabs’ realtime speech models.

There are practical controls here that I understand better after talking to ChatGPT so often. [ElevenLabs lets developers choose](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-agents/customization/conversation-flow) how patiently an agent waits through silence and whether a user can interrupt its reply. A voice can sound excellent and still feel exhausting if it jumps in every time I pause to think. That timing is part of the product.

### Alibaba and Qwen

Alibaba’s [Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash-Realtime](https://qwen.ai/blog?id=qwen3.8-omni-flash) takes the conversation beyond sound. Qwen says it can receive live audio and video, respond while that stream is arriving, and call tools using what it sees and hears. The team has also published an [open-source harness](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen-Live-Harness) that connects to its Realtime API for desktop audio and visual interaction.

Qwen belongs here because it changes the question a user can ask. If I point a camera at something and say “What am I looking at?”, the assistant has to join my words to the image in front of it. A fluent spoken answer is only useful if it refers to the right object. Voice becomes more interesting when it shares some of the context I have while I am speaking.

### ByteDance

Qwen brings in sight. ByteDance’s [Seeduplex](https://seed.bytedance.com/en/blog/introducing-seed-full-duplex-speech-llm-attentive-listening-robust-interference-suppression-enabling-more-natural-interaction) brings the focus back to timing and noise. ByteDance says its model listens while speaking and uses both the sound and the meaning of speech to decide whether a pause is the end of a turn. It also aims to ignore bystanders and background audio so it does not answer the wrong person. The company says Seeduplex is in the Doubao app, and [Volcengine documents](https://docs.volcengine.com/docs/doubao-voice/end-to-end-real-time-voice-full-duplex-version?lang=zh) a full-duplex API for developers.

That work addresses something I notice in everyday voice chats: an interruption can be deliberate, but a pause might only mean I am searching for a word. ByteDance reports fewer premature replies and false interruptions in its own tests. I would treat those as company results until I could hear how the model handles my own speech and surroundings.

### MiniMax

I finish with [MiniMax Speech 2.8](https://www.minimax.io/news/minimax-speech-28) because the sound of the answer still matters. MiniMax’s text-to-speech model can add breaths, hesitations, and other vocal cues, and its [WebSocket API](https://platform.minimax.io/docs/api-reference/speech-t2a-websocket) can stream generated audio. It also supports voice cloning. Those are capabilities for producing speech; by themselves they do not decide when to listen or what answer is correct.

MiniMax makes that distinction useful. When a voice assistant sounds impressive, I want to ask which part impressed me. Was it the timing, the understanding, or the voice reading the reply? A developer may choose different systems for those jobs. Hearing a more expressive answer does not mean the whole conversation has improved.

The same ordinary conversation—a pause, an interruption, a changed question, and some background chatter—would tell me more about these services than separate polished demos.

## What I listen for now

I pay attention to how long I wait before hearing a useful answer. I notice whether the assistant talks over me, whether it lets me interrupt, and whether it understands the question after I change it. If a voice app uses a tool to look something up, I also want its spoken answer to match what the tool actually found.

Those are things developers can check. Try different speakers, long pauses, corrections, and noise in the room. Measure when the first useful audio arrives, how often the assistant interrupts, and whether the final answer is correct. [OpenAI’s evaluation guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-agents) recommends looking at both conversational timing and the outcome of the task.

I keep coming back to ChatGPT Voice because I can think out loud. I want more software to let people speak that way, with pauses and corrections, and still give them an answer they can rely on.
